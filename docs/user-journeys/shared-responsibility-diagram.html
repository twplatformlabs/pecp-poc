How to Read It

Four things worth saying out loud when this goes in front of a room.

What the picture is saying

The boxes never change. Both diagrams contain exactly the same capabilities in the same positions. Only their colour, and the height of the line, differ.
Hatched is not empty. A hatched box is work the team is doing today, somewhere else. Phase 2 is the platform absorbing that load — not filling a gap.
Two boxes never move. Application code and the build pipeline stay with the consumer in both phases. The platform supplies runners and a registry, and deliberately declines to own the pipeline.
The shared strip sits on the line, not beside it. Those items are genuinely joint — one side declares, the other constrains.
One box in the platform block is teal. GitOps delivery is shared: the platform creates the repo and wires Argo to watch it, but what “deploy” means stays with the team. Neither side can ship without the other.

Questions a stakeholder will ask

“So the platform barely does anything in Phase 1?” It does one thing well — it becomes the record of who owns what across GitHub. That record is the precondition for everything in Phase 2.
“Why don't you own the build?” Because owning the pipeline would mean owning every team's toolchain. Instead the platform verifies the output at the production gate — which is why the consumer keeps the evidence box.
“What happens to the hatched work?” It moves below the line. That is the Phase 2 business case in one image.
“What is still undecided?” Several boundaries — observability, on-call, cost, network policy and quota. The detailed model lists fourteen.
