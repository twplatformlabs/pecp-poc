Read this first Nothing is “not offered” to the consumer — it is only not offered by the platform. Where Phase 1 has no platform capability, the responsibility does not vanish; it sits wholly with the team, on infrastructure they already run. 34 of Phase 1's 53 capabilities are carried alone in exactly this way. That reframes Phase 2: the platform is not filling blanks, it is absorbing load the team was already bearing — taking provisioning, wiring and the production gate, while the consumer keeps application code, the build, and the trigger.

What Shifts, Domain by Domain

Phase 1 → Phase 2
The one-minute read. Eleven domains reduced to the sentence that describes how the line moved.
A · Access
One conditional gate, on team creation.
→
A per-action RBAC check, and the team lead becomes a standing approver.
B · Source & code
Repos are consumer-owned, platform observes. Code is wholly theirs.
→
Code unchanged. Repos re-anchor to the Project; the platform owns the GitOps repo.
C · Build & CI
Wholly the team's. The platform maintains a docs page.
→
Still the team's. The platform adds a registry and credentials — never the pipeline.
D · Artifacts
Wholly the team's registry, scanning and signing.
→
Platform provides and isolates the registry; consumer still produces the proof.
E · Environments
Wholly the team's — their clusters, their capacity, their runtime.
→
Almost wholly platform. Runtime stays consumer, but its resilience config is undefined.
F · Secrets
Wholly the team's store, values and delivery.
→
Platform owns store and delivery; consumer owns the values — with an audit question on one lane.
G · Data
Wholly the team's databases, schema and backups.
→
Platform provisions, consumer owns the data — approval depends on which lane leaves a trail.
H · Networking
Wholly the team's ingress, DNS and certificates.
→
The most lopsided domain: consumer picks a name, platform does the rest.
I · Delivery
Wholly the team's deploy mechanism and promotion.
→
Platform owns the wiring, consumer owns the trigger, gate sits at prod.
J · Observability
Wholly the team's — explicitly out of scope.
→
Least resolved. The team loses the runtime but the platform has not committed to replacing the tooling.
K · Compliance
Backstage. Evidence captured quietly; nothing blocks.
→
On-stage at prod. The platform gains a veto — over consumer-produced proof only.

Constants & Questions to Resolve

what holds · what this exercise exposed
Five principles hold the line steady. Twelve questions would move it — and most of them only became visible once the capabilities were split finely enough to ask.

Constants across both phases

Hold
The consumer always owns the application code. Every other row moves. This one never does, in either phase.
Hold
The platform never owns the build pipeline. Runners in Phase 1, a registry and credentials in Phase 2 — but authoring CI stays with the team throughout. It is the only capability the platform actively declines.
Hold
Not offered is not the same as not needed. Every Phase 1 gap is a load the team is already carrying somewhere else. Phase 2 absorbs work rather than filling blanks.
Hold
The platform engine is the record, not a proxy. Central, up-to-date state across integrated systems rather than sitting in the request path.
Hold
The boundary is drawn by evidence, not by risk. The datastore lanes prove it — the same database is self-service with a PR trail and approval-gated without one.

Questions to resolve

B3
Which repository settings are constrained, and which stay open? A team's compliance or security classification decides a subset for them and leaves the rest to the consumer. The membership of each subset, the enforcement mechanism, and how a classification maps to concrete settings are all undrawn.
A4
Is SSH going to be offered? PAT over HTTPS and the gh CLI work today; SSH needs a Security discussion, and the intent is to offer it. The onboarding map has been corrected to match — the platform provisions no credentials.
C2
Are the CI runners GitHub-hosted or a self-hosted pool? The single question that decides whether the platform owns compute in Phase 1 or owns a documentation page. Everything about capacity, patching, contention and cost follows from it.
C1
Does the platform hold any Actions-level policy? Allowed-actions lists, org rulesets, required workflows. A common platform control that no map claims either way.
E4
Who configures workload resilience? Replicas, autoscaling, pod disruption budgets, probes, restart policy. The environment is thoroughly specified; what runs inside it is not.
E5
How does a team request more quota? Defaults are applied but undefined, and no path exists to ask for more.
E6
Who sees and who pays the cost? Once the platform owns the accounts and clusters, attribution, budgets and chargeback become a real boundary.
F4/F5
Who rotates secrets, and are build-time secrets in scope? Rotation is flagged open on the map. Build-time coverage is unresolved — awkward, since the platform issues JFrog push credentials.
G4
Who backs up the database, and who can restore it? The map covers teardown guardrails but not backup, restore, RPO or RTO.
H6
What is the network policy and egress model? The mesh is specified in detail; namespace network policy and egress control are not mentioned at all.
J1
Does the platform provide an observability stack in Phase 2? Datadog appears only as a future integrated system. The team loses direct access to the runtime without a committed replacement.
J2/J3
Who gets paged, and what is the support commitment? Failing workload versus failing cluster, mesh or Argo. Every absorbed capability creates an escalation path that does not exist yet.
I4
Is rollback a git revert or a platform feature — and does it re-pass the gate? Open on the map, and the compliance interaction is unaddressed.
K6
Is two phases of capture-without-exposure acceptable to Compliance? Evidence accumulates from Phase 1; the viewer is Phase 3.
